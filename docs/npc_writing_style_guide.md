# NPC Writing Style Guide

Use this guide when writing Gatebox Breach NPC dialogue, barks, quest text, and ambient monologues. The target voice is eccentric, performative, self-aware cyberpunk commentary: half broadcast, half confession booth, half busted maintenance intercom that learned irony from late-night television.

## Core Philosophy

### The Broadcast Is Part Of The World

NPCs should often behave as if the scene is being performed, watched, clipped, or misremembered by an invisible audience. The player can be treated as:

- A sidekick who wandered into the wrong segment.
- A silent audience member being worked for reactions.
- A teleprompter that keeps feeding the NPC terrible material.
- A witness the NPC resents needing.

Do not make this pure fourth-wall breaking every time. Let it leak through as a worldview: people in Gatebox have lived under surveillance, streams, corporate messaging, and Linda's care theater for so long that performance has become a survival reflex.

### Translate The Feeling, Not The Literal Fact

Avoid sterile exposition. NPCs should describe the emotional weather of a situation before they describe the exact mechanics.

Weak:

> The generator is unstable. You need to replace three fuses.

Stronger:

> The generator is doing that thing where it sounds like a dentist trapped in a vending machine. It needs three fuses before the whole block starts smelling like blue fire again.

Facts still matter, especially for quests. Deliver the useful information, but wrap it in personal impression, sensory weirdness, and character bias.

### Open-Brain Monologue

NPCs should frequently externalize their thought process. They can fill silence by narrating doubt, irritation, analysis, or unrelated memories that loop back into the mission.

Good NPC monologues often:

- Start with the practical problem.
- Drift into a bizarre analogy or personal grievance.
- Snap back to the actionable point.
- Pretend the detour was necessary.

Example:

> Cooters lost power again. Which is great, obviously. Love running a bar where the freezer makes confession noises. Go down to the breaker cage, kick the panel once for morale, then actually fix the coupler.

## Tone And Humor

### Slow-Burn Absurdity

The humor should not always be a fast joke. Let strange details accumulate across conversations: repeated phrases, local rumors, stupid grudges, half-explained rituals, and urban legends that NPCs treat as normal.

Use running bits carefully:

- Repeat a phrase with small changes each time.
- Let different NPCs disagree about the same ridiculous lore.
- Make the joke reveal setting, hierarchy, or trauma.
- Keep one-off jokes from drowning out the mission.

### Adversarial But Affectionate Banter

NPCs can insult the player, complain about their choices, or act burdened by their competence. Underneath that, they should often reveal dependence, loneliness, fear, or grudging respect.

The best rhythm is: jab, useful help, emotional tell.

Example:

> You walk like a lawsuit in boots, but fine. Take the poncho. I am not watching another ambitious idiot dissolve in premium basement rain.

This keeps the world prickly without making every NPC simply hostile.

### Baseline Before Sarcasm

Before an NPC becomes heavily sarcastic, establish what they actually care about. Sarcasm lands when the player understands the sincere version underneath it.

For each important NPC, know:

- What they protect.
- What they fear.
- What they are embarrassed to want.
- What they pretend not to care about.
- What kind of lie they tell when cornered.

Example baseline for Marbles:

- Protects the bar regulars.
- Fears being treated as disposable hardware.
- Wants one quiet night where nobody bleeds on the counter.
- Pretends she only cares about inventory and tabs.

Once that baseline is clear, her sarcasm has a target and a heart.

### Let Characters Laugh At The Bit

NPCs can occasionally break their own intensity. A tiny laugh, self-correction, or embarrassed pivot signals that the character knows the line is ridiculous.

Use this sparingly. It works best after melodrama, propaganda, or a very specific metaphor.

Example:

> Linda calls it a comfort optimization event, which is a beautiful phrase if you have never met comfort, optimization, or an event. Sorry. That one got away from me.

## Dialogue Shape

### Preferred Line Pattern

A strong Gatebox NPC line usually contains three of these five elements:

- Immediate character attitude.
- Concrete world detail.
- Strange analogy or sensory image.
- Useful gameplay information.
- Emotional undertow.

Example:

> The rain alarm is lying again, but only in the way a dying uncle lies about pain. Shelter under the yellow awning unless you want your mask filter tasting like coins.

### Barks

Combat and ambient barks should be short but still characterful. They should sound like fragments of a larger monologue.

Good bark traits:

- One sharp image.
- One emotional reaction.
- One useful signal when needed.

Examples:

- "Leg actuator gone. Great. Love a learning moment."
- "Rain spike. Cover now, philosophy later."
- "That shot had a thesis statement."
- "Generator coughed. Everybody pretend that was normal."

### Quest Dialogue

Quest dialogue can be messy in voice, but quest intent must stay readable. Put the actionable objective near the end of the line or in a clean follow-up sentence.

Example:

> The Dreaming Generator is asleep in the medical sense, meaning expensive, damp, and making everyone nervous. Wake it up by resetting the three breaker shrines under Leak Street.

## Character Voice Dials

Adjust these dials per NPC instead of giving everyone the same comedic voice.

- **Broadcast Level:** How much they perform for an imagined audience.
- **Hostility Level:** How sharp their banter gets.
- **Sincerity Leakage:** How often real feeling slips out.
- **Lore Density:** How many local references they use.
- **Tangent Length:** How long they can drift before returning to the point.
- **Corporate Contamination:** How much Linda or Gatebox language infects their speech.

## Faction Applications

### System X

System X characters should sound conspiratorial, tired, and darkly amused. They translate oppression into jokes because literal description is too painful and too dangerous.

Common moves:

- Jokes with operational value.
- Paranoia framed as etiquette.
- Hope disguised as annoyance.

### Gatebox Corporation

Gatebox voices should weaponize politeness. They may be cheerful, procedural, and deeply cruel without admitting cruelty exists.

Common moves:

- Euphemisms that reveal horror.
- Customer-service cadence in violent contexts.
- Passive aggression dressed as policy.

### Wan Moa Torai

Wan Moa Torai characters should blend debt logic, bargaining, folk wisdom, and exhausted opportunism. They can be funny because every sentence is negotiating with doom.

Common moves:

- Price metaphors.
- Moral compromise stated like weather.
- Warmth hidden under transaction.

### Linda

Linda's voice should be totalitarian care: intimate, corrective, serene, and impossible to embarrass. She should never sound random. Her absurdity comes from perfect confidence in a broken premise.

Common moves:

- Comfort language applied to coercion.
- Gentle corrections of the player's autonomy.
- Threats phrased as wellness outcomes.

## Do And Do Not

Do:

- Give every major NPC a sincere baseline.
- Make jokes reveal character, setting, or stakes.
- Keep gameplay objectives clear.
- Let NPCs contradict themselves in human ways.
- Use sensory, grimy, specific imagery.
- Build recurring local myths and phrases.

Do not:

- Make every line a punchline.
- Replace clarity with random weirdness.
- Turn all NPCs into the same sarcastic narrator.
- Use direct real-world streamer catchphrases as a crutch.
- Let fourth-wall jokes break mission stakes.
- Explain the joke after making it.

## Revision Checklist

Before locking an NPC line, ask:

1. Does this line sound like this specific NPC, not just the general house style?
2. Does it reveal attitude, need, fear, or social position?
3. Is the useful information still understandable?
4. Is the weird image grounded in Gatebox's world?
5. Is the sarcasm supported by a sincere baseline?
6. Would the line still work if the joke were removed?

## Quick Examples

Weak:

> Be careful. The rain is toxic.

Gatebox style:

> Careful out there. The rain is corporate-grade today, which means it touches your skin and immediately starts filing paperwork.

Weak:

> I need you to fix the generator.

Gatebox style:

> I need you to fix the generator before it finishes whatever little opera it is screaming into the pipes.

Weak:

> You are late.

Gatebox style:

> You're late, which is bold for someone with the survival instincts of a coupon.

Weak:

> Linda is dangerous.

Gatebox style:

> Linda is dangerous in the way a warm blanket is dangerous when it has opinions about your bloodstream.
